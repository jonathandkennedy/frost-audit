3

4

17 old URLs (Goose Creek, Mount Pleasant, the attorneys page, the blog, truck and wrongful-death pages) now say “This page could not be found”, yet they still collected 1,164 impressions — 43% of everything the site was shown for. They need redirects and, for the city pages, rebuilding.

5
Basic plumbing is splitting your signals.

Every inner page of the main site shares one title tag; every page of the injury site shares another. 11 main-site pages exist at two or three addresses (with and without www, with and without a trailing slash) and Google is counting them separately. Google's index still shows the main home page as “Remodeling – Coming Soon” from the redesign.

6
Google's AI is already using the main site to answer estate and probate questions.

19 question-style searches — “who handles executor disputes in summerville, sc probate court?”, “which attorneys in summerville, sc handle contested guardianship cases?” — showed the site 180 times at average positions between 4 and 13. These are the sub-questions Google's AI asks itself (its “fan-out”). No page on the site is written to answer any of them yet.

2 · The basics

How Google works, explained simply

Everything in this report uses five words. Here is what they mean, with the picture that goes with them.

What “topical authority” means

Imagine two law firms. Firm A has one page called “Estate Planning”. Firm B has that page, plus a page for wills, one for trusts, one for powers of attorney, one explaining what happens if you die without a will in South Carolina, one about blended families, one about what it costs, one about reviewing an old plan — all linked to each other, all written for Summerville and Dorchester County. The librarian trusts Firm B as the expert. She sends Firm B the hard questions and the easy ones. That trust is topical authority, and it is earned one connected page at a time.

In plain English

Firm A wrote a pamphlet. Firm B wrote the whole shelf. Google hands the shelf to people first. Our plan is to write the shelf — for estate planning, probate and criminal defense on the main site, and for injuries on the injury site.

What Google's AI “fan-out” means (and why it's in your data)

When someone asks Google's AI a big question — “who should handle my dad's estate in Summerville?” — the AI doesn't search that sentence. It quietly splits it into five or ten smaller questions, searches each one, and stitches the answers together. Those smaller questions are called fan-out queries. They show up in Search Console as oddly precise, complete sentences. You already have 19 of them. Section 8 shows how to use them.

Why the Business Profile and Yelp matter

Your Google Business Profile is the sign on the map. It decides whether you are in the three-business map pack, and it already produces more impressions than any page on your website (Section 3). Yelp is a second sign that other assistants read: ChatGPT, Bing and Apple Maps lean on Yelp's listings and reviews when someone asks them for “a good probate attorney near Summerville”. A complete, claimed Yelp listing with the right categories is how you get named in those answers.

5 · The decision

Two websites: the honest pros and cons

You asked for a straight answer on whether to keep both sites. Here is the evidence, the trade-offs in plain language, and the rules that make two sites work if that's the choice.

Why two sites can work

  • Two clear subjects. Google rewards focus. A site that is only about injuries, and a site that is only about estate, probate and criminal, are each easier to make “the shelf” than one site that is about everything.
  • The domain says what it is. “summervilleaccidentattorney.com” matches what injury clients type, and it lets the injury practice look and sound different (urgent, free consultation, 24/7) from the calm, family estate-planning brand.
  • Two chances on the page. When both are strong, a brand search and some local searches can show both domains — two of ten results instead of one.
  • Different audiences, different partners. Estate planning is referred by financial advisors and CPAs; injury work by chiropractors, body shops and past clients. Separate sites make each referral story clean.
  • The main site keeps its head start. Google's AI is already quoting the main site for estate and probate questions; keeping injuries off it protects that focus.

What it costs you

  • Reputation splits. Every link, mention and review builds one domain. The injury site starts from almost nothing and currently averages position 57.4.
  • One Business Profile, one Yelp. A business gets one profile and it links to one website. The injury site is not connected to the map listing that already produces most of your impressions.
  • Double the work. Two sets of pages, titles, redirects, content, videos and monthly checks. The plan below assumes you have the capacity (or a partner) for both.
  • Same firm, same address, same phone on two sites. Google needs to be told clearly that these are one firm with two practice sites, or it may treat one as a copy.
  • Cannibalization is already happening (chart above) and has to be engineered away.
  • Brand confusion. Someone who searched “Frost Law Group” after a crash should land on the injury site, not the estate site — that routing has to be designed.

Our recommendation

Keep both — but only under the seven rules below, and with a checkpoint. The data supports the split: the main site's strength is estate, probate and criminal (that is where Google already trusts it), and the injury market in Summerville is dominated by large firms with big review counts, which a focused, family-run injury site can position against better than a general practice page can. The risks are all manageable if the sites are wired correctly from day one.

The checkpoint: at the end of month six, the injury site should have at least a handful of searches on page one (dog bite, motorcycle, Goose Creek) and its first non-brand clicks every week. If it doesn't, we fold it into the main site as /personal-injury/ with permanent redirects — nothing built is wasted, because every page moves with it.

The seven rules for running two sites

  • One subject per site. The main site's /motor-vehicle-personal-injury page is redirected (301) to the injury site's car-accident page; the “Personal Injury” menu item links across. No injury pages on the main site; no estate, probate or criminal pages on the injury site. Where subjects touch — a DUI charge is criminal (main site), a crash caused by a drunk driver is injury (injury site) — the boundary is written down.
  • Say it's one firm. Identical name, address and phone on both; LegalService structured data on both that lists the other site and the Business Profile in sameAs; attorney bios on both that link to each other.
  • Cross-link in the header and footer with descriptive words (“Our injury practice: Summerville Accident Attorney”), not just a logo.
  • The Business Profile links to the main site (https, no www). The injury site gets its own signals: Avvo, Justia, FindLaw and Super Lawyers profiles for Jack updated to list it; the LinkedIn company page and YouTube channel link both; the Instagram bio uses a link page with both.
  • Separate content calendars, no crossover. Two answer pages a week on the main site, one on the injury site. The injury site also gets the Spanish page and the city pages.
  • Route the brand. The main site's home page gets a visible “Injured in an accident?” panel linking to the injury site; the injury site's header says “A Frost Law Group practice” linking back.
  • Measure them separately, monthly (two Search Console properties, already in place) and hold the month-six checkpoint.
In plain English

Two stores, one owner. Each store sells one thing and says so on the sign. Both stores have the same name on the door and point customers to the other store when they walk into the wrong one. And we agree now: if the second store isn't earning its keep in six months, we merge it into the first — without throwing away anything on its shelves.

  • Title: “[Practice] Attorney in Summerville, SC | Frost Law Group”

Answer page (a spoke)

  • Title is the question, in the words people use
  • First paragraph answers it directly — the sentence Google can quote
  • Then the detail: steps, timelines, costs, statute names, local court
  • Three to five follow-up questions (the next fan-out) answered briefly
  • Links up to the hub and sideways to two related spokes
  • Author and “reviewed on” date; Attorney markup
  • 60–90 second vertical video from Jack or Tara, embedded
  • 800–1,500 words; no filler

Site-wide rules

  • Every page links to its hub; every hub links to every spoke; no page is more than two clicks from the home page.
  • One page per question. If two pages could rank for the same search, merge them.
  • Structured data on every page: LegalService (organization), Attorney (bios), FAQPage (answer blocks), VideoObject (embedded videos), BreadcrumbList.
  • Real photos, real names, real courts. Google's quality guidelines for legal content weight who wrote it and whether they are who they say they are.
  • Publish, then request indexing in Search Console; check impressions on the new page at 14 and 45 days.

8 · AI search

Using Google's fan-out queries to our advantage

Fan-out is the biggest change in how Google works in years, and your Search Console already shows it happening. Here is the playbook.

8.1 Harvest the questions every month

In Search Console → Performance → Queries, filter with a regular expression for question words: ^(who|which|what|how|where|when|can|does|is it|should). Sort by impressions. Every row is a question Google's AI asked on someone's behalf and considered your site for. Copy them into the content calendar.

8.2 Answer each one on its own page, answer-first

The AI is looking for the cleanest 40–60 word answer to that question, from a page that is clearly about that question, on a site that is clearly an authority on the subject. So: the question is the title and the H1; the first paragraph is the answer; the rest of the page earns the trust (statutes, steps, local court, who you are). Add the three to five follow-up questions the AI will ask next as short H2 sections.

8.3 Be the same firm everywhere

AI answers are built from many sources at once: your pages, your Business Profile, Yelp, Avvo, Justia, the SC Bar directory, LinkedIn, YouTube. They check that the facts agree — name, address, phone, attorneys, practice areas. Keep every listing identical and complete, and link them all in your structured data. This is also why Yelp matters for ChatGPT-style answers.

8.4 Put the answer in more than one format

The same answer as a page, as a short video with a transcript, as an FAQ block with markup, and as a Business Profile post gives the AI four consistent places to find it. Video transcripts on YouTube are indexed; a Frost video titled with the exact question is often the only video answer for a Summerville-specific question.

8.5 Worked example: guardianship

Search Console shows “which attorneys in summerville, sc handle contested guardianship cases?” — 39 impressions at an average position of 5.4 — with no guardianship page on the site. The plan: a hub page “Guardianship & Conservatorship Attorney in Summerville, SC”, three spokes (“What is a contested guardianship in SC?”, “How to become a legal guardian in Dorchester County”, “Conservatorship vs. power of attorney”), a four-minute video from Tara, FAQ markup, and a Business Profile post. Then we watch that question move from position 5 to position 1–2, and watch the AI answer start naming the firm.

In plain English

Google's AI is a student writing a report. It doesn't read one book; it asks ten small questions and grabs the clearest paragraph for each. We're going to write the clearest paragraph in Summerville for each of the questions it's already asking — and put your name on every one.

E. Glossary

Impression
One time Google showed a page of yours on a results page (including in the map pack via your Business Profile link and in AI answers).
Click
One time a person chose that result.
Position
Where the result sat on the page, averaged over all the times it was shown. 1–10 is page one.
CTR
Click-through rate: clicks ÷ impressions.
Query
The exact words typed or spoken. Search Console hides very rare queries for privacy, which is why query totals are lower than site totals.
Brand / non-brand
Brand searches contain the firm's name (“frost”). Non-brand searches are people who don't know you yet — the only ones that grow a practice.
Canonical
The one official address for a page. When a page has several addresses, Google picks one and treats the rest as copies.
301 redirect
A permanent forward from an old address to a new one. It sends people and Google's trust to the new page.
Topical authority
Being the site Google trusts most on one subject, earned by covering the subject completely and coherently.
Fan-out
How Google's AI answers a big question: it splits it into smaller questions, searches each, and stitches the best paragraphs together.
Structured data (schema)
Machine-readable labels in the page code that tell Google exactly who you are (LegalService, Attorney), what a block is (FAQPage), and what a video is (VideoObject).
E-E-A-T
Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness, Trust — the qualities Google looks for on legal and financial pages; shown with real authors, credentials, reviews and consistent facts.
